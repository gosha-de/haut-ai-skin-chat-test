# User research: skincare shoppers

Who buys face skincare on brand websites, how their routine changes week by week, and what blocks, scares and pleases them. Read with the week-3 shopper in mind.

## Summary

- **Our shopper is a minority shopper.** Most skincare is bought on Amazon and at retailers. Brand sites are mostly used for research. The shopper who buys in a brand's chat is likely a woman, open to AI, and one of the roughly 1 in 3 people who are comfortable sharing a selfie (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026).
- **Real routines are short.** Most adults use 1–3 products (YouGov, UK, 2025), and only 1 in 5 US adults call their routine "very consistent" (YouGov, US, 2024). A 3-product routine is already above average, so buying 2 of 3 is normal, not a failure.
- **Week 3 is when people judge.** 88% of young shoppers expect results within 4 weeks (Curology, US, 2026, brand). Change for most concerns takes 6–12 weeks. Doubt, irritation and quitting all cluster in weeks 2–4.
- **The top fears are "it won't work" and "it will irritate my skin".** Close behind is the fear of being sold to. People assume a brand's tool is a sales tool.
- **Plain reminders don't help people stick with a routine. Being asked about their own skin does.** This comes from small clinical trials. Good beauty advisors check in at 3 days, 3 weeks and 3 months (Cos Bar, US, vendor case study).
- **People judge results by how their skin feels more than by numbers.** Comfort, tolerance and keeping the routine going are the wins that can show at week 3.
- **A new selfie is a real ask.** Few are comfortable sharing a face photo, photo uploads drop off month by month, and lighting can move a score more than 3 weeks of skincare.
- **"Worse" is emotionally loaded and often not real.** Lighting, early irritation from active ingredients or the season can all cause it. It needs a check first, then a calm next step, including "see a dermatologist".
- **Refill is a real reason to return, but later than week 3 for most products.** A 50 ml cream lasts about a month even at heavy use (Colipa data, EU, 2007), and the typical gap between skincare orders is 2–3.5 months (vendor benchmarks).
- **No source explains why people buy part of a routine.** The likely reasons are price, already owning something similar, doubt that it's needed, and adding one new product at a time. The chat should ask, not re-sell.
- **Being remembered is expected and builds trust,** as long as the chat shows where its knowledge comes from and lets people correct it.

## Who they are

### Where they buy

- Amazon is the #1 online beauty seller in the UK, and 60% of UK online beauty buyers have bought beauty there (NielsenIQ and CEW, UK, 2026). In the US, Amazon and Walmart each start 22% of online beauty searches (Tinuiti, US, 2025, n=1,000+, vendor).
- Brand sites are where people research, less where they buy: 58% call them their main research source, 37% buy there (Stellarising, 2026, vendor, source survey not named).
- So the restock can easily go to a marketplace. What a brand site has that Amazon doesn't is the shopper's own history: scores, routine, what they bought.

### What they are like

- **Mostly women.** 78% of UK women and 42% of UK men bought skincare in the past year (YouGov, UK, 2025, n=2,095). Men are growing: 52% of US men used facial skincare in 2024, up from 31% in 2022 (Mintel, US, 2024).
- **Results come first.** "Effective, proven results" is the top purchase reason for 47% of UK adults and 52% of those 55+. 27% of 18–24s put price first (YouGov, UK, 2025).
- **They trust dermatologists, but use social media.** 43% of 13–29s name dermatologists as their most trusted source, 9% social media. Yet 62% use social media for advice, and half have never seen a dermatologist; cost is the top barrier at 45% (Curology, US, 2026, n=1,930, brand). The chat sits in this gap.
- **Open to AI.** 32% of US women already use AI for skincare or bodycare advice (Haut.AI's own survey, US, 2025–26, n=1,238 women, vendor). 49% of consumers get beauty recommendations from generative AI (NielsenIQ and CEW UK report, 2026).
- **But wary of the selfie.** Only 32% are comfortable sharing a selfie with an AI beauty tool, and 38% their past purchases (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026, n=3,222). Our shopper already shared one. A second one is not a given.
- **Ready to switch.** 31% switched skincare brand in the past year and 61% may switch next year (aytm, US, 2023, n=1,000, vendor). Loyalty follows results (see Good moments).

### What they worry about on their skin

- Acne is a concern for 60% of US under-30s and 13% of those 30+. Wrinkles are a concern for 46% of US women and 18% of men (YouGov, US, 2024, n=1,148). Affluent women 35+ mainly target lines (73%), dryness (51%) and dark circles (47%). Only 15% say their products work "very well" on lines (Drive Research, US/UK/CA/AU, 2023, n=1,004 affluent women, vendor).
- For skin of colour, acne (40%), dryness (32%) and dark spots (26%) lead (multi-centre survey, J Drugs Dermatol, 2022).
- About 4 in 10 adults call their skin very or moderately sensitive (meta-analysis, global, 2020). Self-reports may overstate it.
- What this means at week 3: dryness and comfort can move by now. Lines and dark spots take months, so they rarely move by week 3. See competitor research for the per-concern timeline.

### Segments

Our working list, based on L.E.K.'s segmentation (US/UK/France, 2025, n=3,500+) and the data above. It is not a measured model, and segment sizes are unknown.

| Segment | What they want | What they fear | What it means at week 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Results-seeker** (often women 35+, reads ingredients, spends most) | Proof, the logic behind each product, clinical timelines | Wasting money, no visible change | Switches fast without proof. Needs an honest comparison and "what moves when". |
| **Loyal ritualist** (settled routine, stays longest) | Comfort, easy restock, no hassle | Being upsold, change for its own sake | Wants reassurance and a restock when due. Don't push swaps. |
| **Minimalist** (often men, Gen Z, multi-use fans) | Few steps, a quick answer | Effort, too many products | "Your 2 products are enough for now" can be the right answer. |
| **Young explorer** (13–29, social-led) | Quick wins, something new, low cost | Acne not clearing, cost, how they look | Most likely to have judged already. Needs the timeline reset, short and visual. No anti-ageing push. |
| **Sensitive skin** (about 4 in 10 by self-report) | Calm skin, no reaction | Stinging, redness, breakouts from new actives | Ask about irritation first. "Worse" is more likely real. Add the 3rd product slowly, if at all. |
| **Skin of colour, dark spots** | Even tone | Slow progress, tools that miss dark spots | Long horizon. Don't report "no change in dark spots" as fact (see Fears). Sun protection matters. |

## Their routine and how it changes over time

### What a routine looks like

- **Short.** 39% of UK adults follow 1–3 steps, 11% follow 4–7, 3% more (YouGov, UK, 2025).
- **Cleanser and moisturiser are the only common daily steps.** US adults using each at least daily: moisturiser 32%, cleanser 30%, sunscreen 14%, serum 11%. 63% have never used a serum (YouGov, US, 2024).
- **Women and under-30s do more** (YouGov, US, 2024). Engaged women are the exception: 74% of affluent women 35+ use 3+ products in the morning and 22 minutes a day on skincare (Drive Research, US/UK/CA/AU, 2023, vendor). Our shopper probably sits closer to this group.
- **The trend is toward fewer steps.** In the UK, 25% prefer multi-use products, men more than women (YouGov, UK, 2025).

### How consistent they are

- Only 20% of US adults call their routine "very consistent", 29% "somewhat". 27% have no routine (YouGov, US, 2024). 60% of UK adults lack a consistent routine, and people skip washing their face before bed about twice a week (Kenvue, UK, 2025, n=2,000, brand).
- In acne trials with sensor caps on the tubes, people used their creams about a third of the time over 12 weeks (Boker, US, 2012, n=40). Fewer than 30% stick with acne treatment for a year (Cao review, 2026).
- So at week 3 the shopper is probably using the 2 products, but not every day, and the evening step is the first to slip.

### Week by week

| Weeks 2–4 | Irritation from actives peaks. Doubt peaks. | **Week 3: our shopper** | Uses 2 products, not every day. Sees little change. Wonders whether to go on. | |

### How they decide if it's working

- **By feel first.** In focus groups, people judged products by how they felt on the skin and whether they enjoyed using them, not by measurements (Sagentia, UK, 2019).
- **The mirror hides slow change.** A skin coach's client felt "more or less the same", then reacted "OMG" to her side-by-side photos (Hotline Skin, 2025).
- **"Would I repurchase?" is their own verdict.** One "empties" newsletter rates each finished product yes, maybe or no on results, fit, texture, scent and price (Tender Forms, 2024).
- **Tracking fades.** No source says how many people track their skin. In clinics, home photo uploads fell from 162 of 206 patients at 1 month to 45 at 6 months (Suemitsu, Japan, 2025). No trial shows that photo tracking alone makes people stick with a treatment (Cao review, 2026).

### Seasons and life changes

- 46% of UK adults change part of their routine in winter, and 1 in 10 change it completely (The Body Shop, UK, c. 2024, n=2,000, brand).
- Season is a real reason to return, but on a 3–6 month cycle. At week 3 it is more useful as an explanation for a dip ("redness up, but it's January"). There is no usable data on pregnancy, hormones, stress or travel.

## Blockers

### Confusion

- 85% of women find skincare "packed with jargon". 37% bought trendy ingredients without knowing how to use them. 6 in 10 don't know which ingredients go morning or night. Only 19% know the right order and stick to it (Skin Rocks, likely UK, 2026, n=2,000 women, brand).
- 53% of 13–29s are confused about which products are right for them (Curology, US, 2026, brand).

### Time and money

- 40% of Gen Z say they are too busy for a routine, and 38% feel overwhelmed when buying (Tropic, UK, 2024, brand).
- UK barriers: product cost 30%, low motivation or energy 25%, overwhelmed by advice 20% (Kenvue, UK, 2025, brand). Forgetting is not the main one.
- "Affordable" is very important to 52% of US adults. 49% won't pay more than $30 for one product (YouGov, US, 2024).
- 24% traded down to cheaper beauty products in the past year, and 28% bought dupes (McKinsey, global, 2025).

### The expectation gap

- 88% of 13–29s expect results within 4 weeks or less (Curology, US, 2026, brand).
- Dermatologists say most ingredients need at least a month, acne 4–8 weeks and retinoids 3–4 months (NewBeauty, trade press).
- 83% of young acne patients expected a cream to work within a week. Their top reasons for stopping were "too slow" (44%) and side effects (40%) (Yang, China, 2026, n=501).

### Why not all 3 products (indirect evidence only)

No source measures this. The nearest evidence:
- **Price.** See above. The unbought product may simply be the most expensive.
- **Already own one.** UK adults hold on average 2 unused skincare products (The Body Shop, UK, 2022, n=2,000, brand).
- **Doubt it's needed.** AI scanners tend to suggest long routines: the one Which? tested suggested 9 products by default (Which?, UK, 2026).
- **Adding slowly.** Dermatologists advise no more than one new product every 2 weeks (NewBeauty). 55% fear an allergic reaction to a new product (Drive Research, US/UK/CA/AU, 2023, vendor).
- **Cost, fear of reaction and doubt** are the top barriers to trying any new product (47%, 37%, 36%) (Lab42, vendor, sample and year not stated).

### Why not come back

- No public data on return rates to a brand's skin chat.
- An invitation to re-scan is not reason enough: "I don't know if I'll bother" (Mumsnet, 2023, on going back for an in-store scan).
- 54% of US consumers unsubscribe when a brand keeps pushing the same offer, and 58% would welcome a "pause" option (Optimove, US, 2024, n=329, vendor).
- See competitor research for review evidence on noise and reminders.

## Fears

### It won't work, and the money is wasted

- 87% of affluent women 35+ have some worry about a new product. Top worries: it won't work (62%), an allergic reaction (55%), worse skin (19%). 90% are frustrated by how hard it is to find products that work (Drive Research, US/UK/CA/AU, 2023, vendor).
- Only 5% of US adults think non-prescription skincare is "very effective" at what it claims (YouGov, US, 2024).
- Reasons for abandoning a product: wrong for their skin from the start (27%), found something newer (26%), worked at first then stopped (18%) (The Body Shop, UK, 2022, brand).

### It will make my skin worse

- 29% of women got irritation or breakouts after using a product they didn't fully understand (Skin Rocks, likely UK, 2026, brand).
- Retinoid irritation is concentrated in the first weeks and leads people to stop (systematic review, 2025). Experts say mild irritation is expected and not a reason to stop on its own; spacing out use helps (Iberian dermatology consensus, 2024).
- "Purging" (breakouts from a new active in weeks 2–4) is widely described but contested. One review of trial data finds no separate purging phase (Vegalab, brand, citing a 2009 study). See competitor research for the usual timeline.
- An AI scanner suggested retinol and salicylic acid to a tester with rosacea (Which?, UK, 2026). Teens copy routines with an average of 11 irritating ingredients (Pediatrics, US, 2025).

### Being sold to, and trusting AI

- The top worry about AI beauty advice is paid placement: 43% fear it. 45% want sponsored recommendations disclosed (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026).
- In Which?'s test, two brands dominated the recommendations, and 25 of 30 testers got completely different products after changing their quiz answers (Which?, UK, 2026).
- Consultations feel like sales: "They're all trying to sell you something aren't they" (Mumsnet, 2024).
- Trust in AI advice is lowest for acne and sensitive skin, and lower in the UK. UK trust: moisturiser 60%, sensitive skin 53%, acne 46%. 73% would trust AI more if it had been tested across skin tones, ages and genders (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026).
- Skincare has a history of hidden subscriptions. The US FTC refunded over $27.6m for repeat-shipment plans that included clear-skin products (FTC, US, 2025).

### Sharing the face, and being remembered

- US comfort with face scans fell from 44% (2022) to 33% (2024) (GetApp, US, 2024, n=1,000, vendor).
- AI advice feels invasive if it stores data without clear consent (44%) or uses a selfie in an unexpected way (43%). 79% would be uncomfortable if AI guessed a personal-care need they hadn't told it (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026).
- Charlotte Tilbury paid $2.925m to settle a US (Illinois) case over its online skin analysis (2024–25).
- See competitor research for older surveys on when memory feels "creepy".

### The score is wrong

New evidence from the Which? test (UK, Oct 2026, 30 testers, 120+ scans). See competitor research for its 63% match rate and repeat-scan stability.
- **Changing the light moved scores by +39 to −25 points** for about half the testers.
- Acne was missed or contradicted for 10 of 14 testers who had it. Dark spots were rated "healthy" for 6 of 9. Redness was flagged for 0 of 7.
- On darker skin (Fitzpatrick IV–V), dark spots were often missed and overall scores came out higher.
- Haut.AI's own benchmark names false alarms and missed problems as the two main accuracy issues (SkinBench, vendor).

### Being judged

- 42% find AI advice invasive if it suggests they need to "fix" how they look. 85% want AI to help people feel confident, not judged (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026).
- Acne is linked to higher anxiety and depression (meta-analyses: JAAD 2020, Cureus 2023).
- 1 in 3 people aged 13–29 have cancelled plans because of how they felt about their skin (Curology, US, 2026, brand).
- Beautifying filters on your *own* face hurt body image more than seeing them on others (Computers in Human Behavior, 2024).

## Good moments and what makes them stick

### What feels good

- **The daily ritual.** 95% of moisturiser users say emotional benefits matter. Applying skincare beat a compliment, meditation and coffee as a mood booster. People who call it a "ritual" rather than a "routine" feel more confident (Clinique, US/UK/Mexico/Japan/Spain, 2024, n=5,012, brand).
- **Good skin days.** 55% of US adults feel confident when their skin is at its best (Ipsos for Bodewell, US, 2022, n=2,010, brand).
- **Skin that feels nice, and compliments.** Both come up often in first-person writing, but no survey measures them.
- **Finding something that works** after a lot of trying, and wanting to rebuy it.
- **Seeing your own face change.** A side-by-side photo can show change the shopper didn't feel. In a trial, a face-ageing selfie app raised daily sunscreen use from 15% to 23% after 6 months (Brinker, Brazil, 2020).

### What makes them stick

| What was tried | Result | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Text reminders twice a day, 12 weeks | 34% use vs 37% without: no help | Boker, US, 2012, n=40 |
| Daily phone reminders | 48% vs 59% with nothing: no better | Yentzer pilot, US, c. 2010, n=61 |
| Frequent clinic visits | 82%, the best arm in the same pilot | Yentzer pilot |
| A weekly online survey about their own acne | About 74–89% vs 32–33% without | Yentzer, US, 2011, n=20 (two sources give different figures; teens got small gift cards) |
| An app for psoriasis | 65% vs 38% at week 4, but the gain faded by week 8 | via Cao review, 2026 |
| Leaflet and video on how long results take | "May increase" use | UK, 2017, funded by Galderma |

- One Italian trial found text reminders did help (Fabbrocini, 2014, n=160). Still, a 2014 review concludes that daily reminders don't help, while weekly check-ins and education do. People also use their creams more just before a clinic visit (Feldman, US, 2007, n=29).
- The researchers called the weekly survey a "virtual office visit". The point is being asked about their own skin, not being told to use a product. All of these are small trials on prescription creams, so applying them to cosmetic routines is our inference.

### The advisor's rhythm, and being remembered

- Cos Bar, a US beauty retailer, trains advisors to follow up **3 days, 3 weeks and 3 months** after a purchase (Salesfloor case study, vendor).
- The 3-day call asks how the skin feels. Our week-3 moment matches what good human advisors already do.
- 91% are more likely to shop with brands that recognise and remember them (Accenture, 8 countries, 2018, n=8,000; older). 76% get frustrated when personalisation fails (McKinsey, US, 2021).
- Which? testers trusted a tool more when it suggested "the sunscreen I actually use".
- About 70% would trade some privacy for more relevant AI beauty advice (FIT whitepaper, US, 2025, n=500+). It only works if the relevance is visible.

### What earns the next purchase

- Skincare loyalty is high, but it rests on results. 55% say quality drives loyalty, 30% value. Nearly half would switch over performance (Provoke Insights, US, 2023–26, vendor).
- Loyalty programmes barely matter: 7% call them their most important driver. About 8 in 10 say ingredient and benefit information matters to what they buy (AlixPartners, US, 2025, n=5,000).
- Honest timelines build trust: 66% of 13–29s distrust instant-results claims (Curology, US, 2026, brand). 77% would value an AI tool that shows what results to expect (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026).

## Two informal talks

Two women, asked informally in November 2026. This is not a study: 2 people, not recruited, and neither has used an AI skin check, so neither is our shopper. Read it as a check on the desk research.

| | Person 1 | Person 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Skin | Problem skin | Not stated |
| Whom she trusts | Her dermatologist, seen often | Herself, a dermatologist-blogger on Instagram, ChatGPT |
| AI skin checks or apps | Never | Never. Once sent skin photos to the dermatologist-blogger |
| How she picks products | Sticks with what she trusts, tries new ones now and then | Bloggers' picks and gifts. Asks ChatGPT about ingredients and country of origin before buying |
| A new product that doesn't suit | Irritates her skin within days | Wrong smell or feel: she gives it away |
| Refill | When it's about to run out. Knows how long each lasts | When it's about to run out. Doesn't track |

What it adds:
- **Both share their skin with a person, not a scanner.** This matches the desk research: dermatologists are the most trusted source, and few are comfortable sharing a selfie with an AI tool. When the shopper has a dermatologist, the chat should defer, not compete.
- **"Does it suit me?" is decided in days, by feel.** Irritation or the wrong smell or feel ends it fast. This backs an early check-in in the first days. Week 3 answers a different question: "is it working?"
- **A failed product disappears quietly.** It is given away, and the brand never learns why. An early check-in is the only chance to catch it and suggest something else.
- **Once a routine is settled, refill needs no reminder.** Both see the bottle running low. A reminder helps most in the first cycle of a new product, when the shopper doesn't know yet how long it lasts. That is our week-3 shopper. What helps at refill is the choice "same again, or adjust?", not the alert.
- **AI chat is used to check a product before buying:** ingredients and origin, other brands included. That is a reason to come back the brief doesn't name, and it backs "products from other shops".
- **Routines are mixed from many sources:** gifts, bloggers' picks, other brands. A brand's chat sees only part of the routine.

## What this means for the design

Each point says which ideas in ideas.md the research backs or challenges.

1. **Make week 3 a check-in about their skin, not a reminder.** Plain reminders didn't help in trials, and daily calls did no better than nothing. Being asked about their own skin helped. Human advisors already follow up at 3 weeks. And week 3 is when people decide whether to go on.
   - Backs: the "Is it working?" check-in, "first message is about them, not a sale", "reminders without guilt".
2. **Open with what the chat knows, then ask one question before telling.** Recognition builds trust ("the sunscreen I actually use"). Only 38% are comfortable sharing past purchases (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026), so say where the knowledge comes from.
   - Backs: "the chat opens with their own context", "a few short questions instead of a form", "say why the chat knows something", "let them see and reset what is remembered".
3. **Reset expectations: "week 3 of about 8".** 88% of young shoppers expect results within 4 weeks (Curology, US, 2026, brand); change takes 6–12. Honest timelines are something people say they want, not a weakness.
   - Backs: "expectation timeline per concern", "nothing changed: on track for week 3, with the date when change can show".
   - Adds: don't over-claim on dark spots, especially on darker skin. Scanners often miss them, so a flat score may be a miss, not "no change".
4. **Count the wins that can show by week 3: feel, comfort, tolerance, keeping it going.** People judge by feel first. Missed days don't break a habit, so say so.
   - Backs: "what the shopper felt counts as well as the score", "never blame: ask how the routine went".
   - Adds: a calm "most people skip a few evenings, that's fine" instead of tracking use.
5. **Make the new selfie optional, and explain why it helps.** Few are comfortable sharing a face photo, uploads fade over months, and light swings scores. Week 3 can work without a photo. A photo is worth more at week 8.
   - Backs: "option to skip the selfie", "the last photo as a see-through guide", "a normal variation band".
   - Challenges: any plan that asks for a new selfie at every touchpoint.
6. **For "worse", check before advising, and give a way out.** First check the photo conditions, then ask about stinging or dryness, then the season or stress, then adjust use. For burning, spreading redness or rosacea, suggest seeing a dermatologist. Don't call it "purging" as a fact; it is contested.
   - Backs: the "got worse" order and "ask for context before judging the new photo".
   - Adds: a clear "pause and see a professional" path, especially for sensitive or acne-prone skin.
7. **Treat the 3rd product as a question, not an abandoned cart.** 1–3 steps is normal, many already own something similar, and experts advise adding one product at a time. Ask why, offer "I already have something similar" and "keep 2 for now", and never suggest it while the skin is irritated. Say why each product is there; restraint is what beats the sales suspicion.
   - Backs: "the 3rd product, if progress suggests it helps", "products from other shops", "the swap suggestion must not feel like an ad", and parking "loyalty points" (only 7% care about programmes; AlixPartners, US, 2025).
   - Challenges: the product-page nudge "This completes your routine". It treats 2 of 3 as unfinished.
8. **Make refill the second reason to return, not the first.** A 50 ml cream lasts about a month even at heavy use (Colipa data, EU, 2007). Typical reorder gaps are 2–3.5 months (vendor benchmarks). A cleanser may run out near week 4, a serum nearer week 8 (vendor estimates).
   - Backs: "refill timed per product, from its size and daily use".
   - Challenges: "one message that combines the refill and the progress check, sent around day 21", and the competitor-research gap "week 3 is refill time". This only holds for small or fast-used products.
9. **Plan the next check-in, and the end.** The benefit of contact fades when contact stops. Repeated offers drive unsubscribes, and most people welcome a pause. A rhythm like the advisors' (a few days, 3 weeks, about 3 months) has a natural end.
   - Backs: "book the next check-in for a date when change can show", the stop rules, "add to calendar".
10. **Use kind, plain words, and show the real face only.** "Fix" language feels invasive, alarming scores feel like an attack, and acne is tied to anxiety.
    - Backs: the whole "people are fragile, be gentle" section, including "no anti-ageing push to the young".
    - Challenges in part: "expected result as a reason to take the next selfie". Seeing your own face does motivate, but a perfected version of your own face hurts body image. Keep the timeline; drop the simulated face at week 3, or show it only on request.
11. **Season is a later reason to return, not the week-3 one.** It works as an explanation for a dip now, and as a touchpoint in 3–6 months. Only 30% are comfortable sharing location or climate with an AI beauty tool (Harris Poll, US/UK/BR/IN, 2026).
    - Backs: the seasonal idea and its watch-out that the address needs consent or a clear notice.

## Assumptions we still have to make

No source answered these. Each goes into the rationale.

- **Why the shopper bought 2 of 3.** We assume price, already owning something similar, or waiting to see results. The chat asks rather than guesses.
- **Which product was not bought, and whether the routine has an active** like retinol or an acid. This decides how likely irritation is at week 3, and whether the missing product could be a soothing step.
- **How fast each product runs out.** Usage data exists only for face cream.
- **Who uses skin analysis on brand sites.** We assume an engaged adult woman who is open to AI. There is no data by age or country.
- **That trial results transfer.** The check-in evidence comes from small prescription acne and psoriasis trials, mostly 2007–2014.
- **That week 3 is a decision point.** The figures that support it are brand surveys or unverified.
- **That shoppers want to come back to a brand's chat at all.** There is no public data on return rates.
- **How people react to "no change" or "worse" scores.** No study measures it.
- **That the shopper is an adult.** Teens use these tools too. We don't design an age check.
- **That a 3-week follow-up feels good to shoppers, and how many check-ins are too many.** Only retailers report on follow-ups, and no data sets a limit.

## Limits of this research

- Desk research, plus two informal talks with women who have never used an AI skin check. No interviews or tests with real shoppers of a brand's chat.
- Many numbers come from brand or vendor surveys, often press releases with the method not shown.
- Most data is from the US and UK. Little is EU-specific. A few studies come from China, Brazil, Japan and Türkiye and are used only where they add something.
- The trial evidence on sticking with a routine is small, often old and about prescription creams.
- Reddit could not be read directly. First-person voice comes mostly from Mumsnet, which skews UK, female and 35–55. Retinoid users are over-represented in week-by-week stories, and their early weeks are harsher than a typical brand routine.
- No first-person voice from men or teens was found.
- Before anything goes on a slide: check quotes on their pages (they were collected with an automated page reader), check Harris Poll label pairings against its charts (read from PDF text), and treat "unverified" figures as search-snippet only.
- No data on brand-site buyers by age or country, on repeat use of skin analysis, or on why people buy part of a routine.
